Rainwater Harvesting Studio — Method Notes

What this tool calculates, which clause of the standard each step comes from, and — the part that matters most when you cite it — where it departs from that standard and where it uses figures with no source at all. Everything below is read from the source code and checked against BS EN 16941‑1:2024. Where the code makes something unclear, this page says so rather than inferring.

Quick start

  1. Open the app and let the tour run, or dismiss it. Models › Terraced housing row loads a worked example: five blocks, 325 m² of catchment, 18 occupants, Dublin rainfall.
  2. In the System panel tick Daily balance. Leave it off and the store is capped at one tank-full a month, which is a property of the time step, not of the tank (see How exact the numbers are).
  3. Set Storage capacity to Set manually and type 1000, then 2000. Watch the coverage rate in the Results panel: 49.5 % then 68.8 %.
  4. Type 4000. Still 68.8 %. You have just found the point where more tank buys nothing.
  5. Export › Explain sheet walks the same numbers step by step and names which one is the answer. Export › Report sheet is the A3 version for a crit.

The one number to report is the mains water displaced — what the store actually supplied. The annual yield is what the roof collects; a good deal of it overflows. Quote yield without storage and you will overstate the benefit, often by half.

Three things to know before you cite a number

  1. The tool holds no daily rainfall, anywhere, at any point. BS EN 16941‑1:2024 A.2.2.2 requires the detailed approach to run on measured daily rainfall over at least five years. This tool has twelve monthly totals per location and nothing else. Its daily balance option does not read daily data: it divides the month's depth by a typical depth per wet day to get a wet-day count, then spreads the month's inflow evenly across those synthetic days. Every storage result here is therefore an approximation of the detailed approach, not the detailed approach. Do not present a tank size from this tool as a BS EN 16941‑1 detailed-approach sizing.
  2. Two of the twenty-eight locations are not on the same footing. The library is sourced, but not uniformly. Twenty-two cities come from the WMO Climate Normals 1991–2020 — gauge measurements from a named station, one period and one definition of a rain day throughout. Six — Nairobi, Athens, Amman, Baghdad, Doha and Bogotá — come instead from the WMO World Weather Information Service, because their countries contribute no station to the Normals composite. Those six carry whatever period their national service published, ranging from 1955–1997 to 1976–2008, and two of them declare no period at all. docs/climate-source.json names the source, station and period for every city. Cite that file rather than this sentence, and check which column your city is in before you quote its rainfall.
  3. Depth per wet day is on the tool's own footing for twenty-two cities, and not for six. This tool counts a wet day as 1 mm or more. The WMO Climate Normals parameter it divides by, DP01, is defined by the archive as the number of days with at least 1 mm of precipitation — the same threshold, and the one WMO-No. 1203 names as the principal precipitation-frequency parameter, so every country's submission to that file is on it. For those twenty-two, depth per wet day is the published annual rainfall divided by a day-count defined exactly as this tool defines one. The six on WWIS are not: Baghdad counts a rain day at 0.001 mm, and four of the six declare no threshold at all. A lower threshold counts more days, so the depth per day comes out lower, and the daily step is built on that figure. It is recorded per city rather than converted, because there is no honest way to convert it. Comparing the daily-step reliability of Baghdad with that of Berlin is not comparing like with like; comparing Berlin with London is.
  4. A station is not a city. Every preset on the Normals is one gauge, recorded by name, WMO station number and elevation. London in that file is the station at 51.505, −0.131 — WMO 00003770, 5 m — reading 113.5 rain days a year. Heathrow is a different row 22 km west, WMO 00003772 at 25 m, reading 111.6. Both are real and both are London in ordinary speech. This tool uses the first; if you quote a figure, quote the station, not the city. Several cities are represented by the nearest gauge rather than one bearing their name — Kuala Lumpur by Subang at 2 km, Jakarta by Stasiun Meteorologi Kemayoran at 5 km, Istanbul by Sariyer at 20 km — and the distance is recorded for each. Bogotá shows the limit of that: its nearest gauge is 12 km away and reads 64 % wetter, because the city sits at 2,600 m and the gauge does not, so it was refused and the city kept its older record.
  5. The litres-per-second figure is not a design. The gutter and downpipe check uses a 75 mm/h storm and a seven-row pipe capacity table, and the code names a source for neither. Its own comment marks it as not a substitute for BS EN 12056‑3. Use it to see that a roof this size needs pipes of roughly this order, and for nothing else.
01

What it calculates

The catchment is the plan projection, and that is the standard's own definition

The tool builds each roof as a set of planes, takes the area vector of every face by Newell's method, and keeps the vertical component. That is the area of the roof's shadow on the ground, not the area of the sloping surface you would tile.

The standard behind the tool is BS EN 16941‑1:2024, which superseded BS 8515:2009 — the code says so, and that phrase is the only place BS 8515 appears in it; nothing the tool calculates comes from the older code of practice.

This is not a simplification the tool has chosen. BS EN 16941‑1:2024, clause 6.1.2, defines A in Formula (1) as the horizontal projection of the collection area. Rain falls vertically; a steeper roof intercepts no more of it.

Everything follows from this, and it is the single most useful thing the tool teaches:

At 45° you are buying 41 % more roof covering and collecting exactly the same water. A barrel vault is the extreme case in this tool: on the same footprint its surface reaches 185.7 m² against a catchment of 117.0 m².

Catchment for each roof form

With footprint w × d and overhang o, seven of the eight forms give the same catchment and one does not:

flat, gable, hip, shed, butterfly, vault, pyramid A = (w + 2o)(d + 2o) sawtooth A = (w + 2o)(d + o) The sawtooth carries its overhang on the low edge only, so it loses one overhang's depth. On a 12 x 8 m block with o = 0.5 m that is 110.5 m2 against 117.0 m2.

The yield equation, as implemented

The code implements Formula (1) of clause 6.1.2 directly, summed over surfaces so that blocks with different materials each keep their own coefficient:

Y = SUM( A_i x e_i x h x eta ) litres A_i horizontal projection of collection area i, m2 e_i surface yield coefficient of that surface, Table 2 h rainfall depth for the time step, mm eta hydraulic treatment efficiency coefficient 1 mm falling on 1 m2 = 1 litre, which is where the units close.

Worked, for the 12 × 8 m gable above: 117.00 m² × 615 mm × 0.80 × 0.90 = 51 841 litres a year, the 51.84 m³ in the table above.

Two losses, and only two

e, the surface yield coefficient, is what the roof surface itself loses to wetting, evaporation and wind. The tool offers all eight surfaces of Table 2 at the published values — see How the roof is generated.

η, the hydraulic treatment efficiency coefficient, is what the pre-tank treatment loses. The tool defaults to 0.90. The standard's note to clause 6.1.2 says that where the manufacturer does not specify a figure, 0.9 can be used for a system with no additional treatment. It is a default to fall back on, not a minimum the standard requires, and the tool's earlier wording said otherwise. Put the manufacturer's figure in when you have one.

The first-flush input is this tool's own addition. Formula (1) has no first-flush term. The tool subtracts the diverted depth from each synthetic wet day before applying the coefficients. Leave it at 0 to calculate strictly to the standard.

The demand side

Demand is built per person per day from four end uses, multiplied by occupancy, plus an optional irrigation demand applied only within a chosen season:

Irrigation is area × mm/week × days/7, applied in whole months between the chosen start and end. The standard treats this as the non-person-related demand Df,d of Formula (2); the tool's weekly rate and season are its own parameterisation, and it has no evapotranspiration model behind them.

Annual demand is daily demand × 365, as Formula (4). Note 2 of that clause points out that 365 suits residential premises and that commercial or public buildings may run on fewer days. The tool always uses 365 and gives you no way to say otherwise, so a school or office model overstates annual demand by the days the building is closed.

The store, and which number is the answer

The store is operated by Formulas (A.3) and (A.4) of Annex A.2.2.3. Abstraction is the lesser of the demand and the volume that was in the store at the end of the previous step, before this step's inflow is added; anything above the usable volume overflows. That convention is called YAS, Yield After Spillage, and it is the conservative one. The tool runs three warm-up cycles through the year before reporting the fourth, so the answer does not depend on how full the tank was assumed to be on 1 January.

That second figure is the tool's own; the standard has no such measure, and the two diverge whenever a shortfall is partial rather than total.

02

How the roof is generated

A model is a set of rectangular blocks. Each block has a footprint, a wall height, a roof form and a surface. The roof is generated parametrically from the form: six of the eight are extruded along X from a profile drawn in the (z, y) plane, and two — hip and pyramid — are built radially on the expanded footprint.

What is fixed

Blocks are rectangular on plan, with vertical walls and a level eaves line. There are no L-shaped or curved footprints, no dormers, rooflights, plant or service penetrations, and no obstruction of one roof by another. Gutters and downpipes are drawn, and downpipes are counted for the drainage check, but neither is modelled as a hydraulic object: nothing in the calculation knows a gutter's profile, fall or length.

03

What is and isn't modelled

Included

  • Catchment as the horizontal projection, per surface, for eight roof forms and any number of blocks.
  • Yield to Formula (1), with the Table 2 coefficient of each surface and a treatment coefficient.
  • Non-potable demand from four editable end uses, occupancy and a seasonal irrigation term.
  • A store operated by the YAS rule of Formulas (A.3) and (A.4), stepped monthly or daily, with three warm-up cycles.
  • Capacity by the basic approach of A.2.1, including the BSI UK National Annex NA.3 reduction, and by reading a knee off the coverage-against-capacity curve of A.2.2.4.
  • Coverage rate Cr to Formula (A.5), overflow, mains top-up and the saving in money at a tariff you set.
  • An indicative gutter and downpipe check, expressly not a BS EN 12056‑3 design.

Not modelled

None of the following is approximated, simplified or estimated. It is absent, and a result from this tool says nothing about any of it:

  • Water quality, treatment and risk assessment — Clauses 5.9 and 10 of the standard, and the whole of the BSI UK National Annex NA.2 with its guideline values, monitoring and sampling. A tool that sizes a tank has said nothing about whether the water in it is fit for the use.
  • The back-up supply — Clause 6.2. The tool reports a mains top-up volume; it does not size the back-up pipe or check the air gap.
  • Pumps, pipework, controls, pressure and energy. No pump curve, no head, no kWh.
  • Carbon, embodied impact, capital cost and payback. The tool multiplies displaced mains water by a tariff. That is a saving in water charges, not a financial appraisal.
  • Rainfall intensity, storm events, snow, and extremes. The store calculation never sees an intensity; the drainage check uses one fixed value.
  • Climate change. The BSI UK National Annex NA.4 asks for a sensitivity analysis on future drier summers and wetter winters, and recommends a seasonal assessment alongside the annual one. The tool has no future scenario and does not prompt for one.
  • Spatial rainfall, wind-driven rain and local shelter. Depth is uniform over the site.
  • Tank geometry, siting and losses. The capacity is a number of litres. There is no shape, no depth, no minimum water level, no calmed inlet, no evaporation or leakage.
  • Gutters and downpipes as hydraulic objects, as noted above.
  • Overflow discharge. The tool reports the volume that overflows and stops there. Where it goes, and whether the drain or the ground can take it, is outside the model.

Regulatory compliance

The tool checks nothing against Building Regulations, water fittings regulations, or any local requirement. It has no approval logic, no pass or fail, and no notion of jurisdiction beyond the BSI UK National Annex rules named on this page. Treat every output as a design study.

04

How exact the numbers are

The daily balance is not daily data

This is the tool's sharpest departure from the standard, and it is worth stating exactly. The detailed approach of A.2.2 runs on measured daily rainfall over at least five years, preferably recent. The tool has twelve monthly totals. When you switch on the daily balance it:

wet days in month = round( monthly depth / typical depth per wet day ) clamped to between 1 and the days in the month then the month's inflow is divided equally between those wet days, spaced evenly through the month.

So a synthetic year of identical showers stands in for a real one of downpours and dry spells. Real rainfall is far lumpier, which means real dry runs are longer and real spills larger than this tool will show. Expect it to flatter a small store. The depth-per-wet-day figure that drives the whole construction is one number per location, and it shares the provenance of the rainfall beside it: the published annual rainfall divided by the published count of precipitation days, from the same station over the same period. A location so dry that no day reaches 1 mm has no wet days at all, so the rule falls back to 8 mm rather than dividing by zero — worth knowing for the driest presets.

There is a second-order consequence worth knowing: first-flush diversion is applied per synthetic wet day, so the first-flush loss is a direct function of a wet-day count the tool invented.

Gauge normals, and a baseline rather than the present

Two properties of the preset data limit every number built on it, and neither is a defect to be worked around — they are what the data is.

It is measurement, but not at your site. These are gauge readings from a named station, which is a stronger footing than the reanalysis this library briefly used: a reanalysis is a physical model run backwards over past observations to produce a gridded estimate, and averages a thunderstorm or a hillside away. A gauge does not. What a gauge cannot do is be somewhere it is not. The station serving your city may be an airport on the edge of it, and the distance is recorded in docs/climate-source.json precisely so you can judge whether it represents the roof you are modelling. Where a record exists for your own site, it remains the better source and you should type it in.

The in-app fetch is a different thing. Fetching rainfall for your own coordinates still queries the Open‑Meteo archive, which serves a reanalysis. So a fetched figure and a preset are not the same kind of number: the preset is a gauge normal, the fetch is a gridded model estimate. The app labels which one produced any figure it prints. Do not quote them as though they were equivalent.

1991–2020 is a baseline, not the present. For the twenty-two cities on the WMO Climate Normals that window is exact, and it is the World Meteorological Organization standard normal period — the convention published climate figures are quoted on, so a preset, a site you fetch and a figure from a met service are all the same kind of thing. For the other six it is whatever their service published, as early as 1955–1997. Either way it is a record of the past. National Annex NA.4 asks a designer to consider how rainfall may change over a system's life; this tool holds no climate scenario and will not prompt you for one, so a tank sized here is sized for the climate that was, not the one that will be.

On a monthly step the store can only deliver one tank-full a month

With monthly stepping there is one draw per month, taken against the volume standing at the end of the previous month. A tank cannot be drawn twice, so however often it would really refill, the most it can supply in a month is its own capacity. Measured on the terraced-housing model, whose demand is about 22 m³ a month:

Where it is conservative, and where it is not

  • Conservative: the YAS rule itself, which meets demand from the previous step's volume and so never lets water arriving today be used today.
  • Conservative: annual demand at 365 days, for any building that is not occupied all year.
  • Not conservative: the synthetic even wet days, which shorten dry runs and understate spill.
  • Not conservative: a catchment with no allowance for anything lost at gutter, filter or overflow beyond the two coefficients.
  • Neither, just wrong at small capacities: the monthly step, as set out above.

The arithmetic itself is exact to floating point, and the volume balance closes: inflow equals supplied plus overflow plus the change in store. Precision in the arithmetic is not accuracy in the answer, and the inputs above are where the uncertainty lives.

06

Compared with the standard, and with a hand calculation

Where it should agree

  • Annual yield, exactly. Formula (1) with a Table 2 coefficient is arithmetic, and the tool does it as written. If you compute A × e × h × η by hand with the same inputs, the tool will match you to the litre. Any disagreement is in A, and it will be because you used the sloping area.
  • Capacity by the basic approach, exactly. A.2.1 is one multiplication and the tool performs it with the dry period you choose, including the NA.3 reduction.
  • The direction and rough scale of every storage result, against a detailed-approach spreadsheet built on Formulas (A.3) to (A.5). The recurrence is the same one.

Where it will diverge

  • Against a detailed-approach simulation on measured daily rainfall, which is what A.2.2 of the standard asks for. The rainfall series is the whole difference: real records have dry runs and cloudbursts that a month divided into equal showers does not. Expect this tool to show shorter deficits and less overflow than the real series would, and the gap to be widest for small stores and seasonal climates.
  • Against a gauge record for your own site, if one exists. The presets are gauge normals from a named station, which is a real source — but that station may be kilometres from the roof you are modelling, and the distance is recorded for each.
  • On any building not occupied 365 days a year, because the tool cannot be told otherwise.
  • On the knee. No standard produces it. Two people reading the same curve will choose different volumes, which is exactly what A.2.2.4 expects — it calls the choice a compromise between coverage, technical constraints and cost.
  • On anything touching quality, pipework, pumps, carbon or cost, where there is nothing to compare because there is nothing modelled.

Against a national average

When the library is regenerated, docs/climate-source.json records beside each city the World Bank's average annual precipitation for that country. It is there to be disagreed with. A country figure is a long-term average over a whole land area, so for a large or varied country it will sit a long way from any particular city — which is the point of looking. Three pairs among the twenty-eight share a country: Kuala Lumpur with Kuching, Seattle with Phoenix, Sydney with Melbourne. In the library as it currently stands they differ by roughly 1 500, 770 and 550 mm a year, and one national number cannot be right for both halves of any of them.

So use it as a sanity check on the order of magnitude and on nothing else. Nothing in the app is calculated from it, and a city figure that differs from its national average is not thereby wrong — Phoenix is supposed to differ from the average of the United States.

Against the tool's own drainage figure

The litres-per-second check divides the catchment by a 75 mm/h storm and looks up a pipe from a seven-row table. A BS EN 12056‑3 design uses the local design intensity, a gutter profile, its fall and length, and a run-off coefficient that the standard's own note to clause 6.1.2 explicitly distinguishes from the surface yield coefficient used here. The two are different coefficients answering different questions. Expect no agreement and seek none.

  1. Open Models › Terraced housing row. Five blocks, 325 m² of catchment, 18 occupants, Dublin rainfall.
  2. In System, tick Daily balance, and set Storage capacity to Set manually.
  3. Record the coverage rate at 500, 1000, 1500, 2000, 4000 and 10000 litres. You should get 24.7 %, 49.5 %, 68.8 %, 68.8 %, 68.8 %, 68.8 %.
  4. Plot them. The first 1 000 litres buys 59 points of coverage. The next 8 500 buys one tenth of a point.
  5. Now switch Storage capacity back to BS basic approach and read the size it proposes: 8 913 litres, against a curve that flattened at 1 500.
  6. Untick Daily balance and watch every figure collapse. Explain why before reading section 04.

The principle. Storage buys reliability with sharply diminishing returns, and the point of diminishing return is set by how often the tank refills — not by the annual totals. A rule of thumb worked out on annual figures cannot see that point: here it sizes the store six times larger than the curve justifies. Size from the curve, and say which time step you ran it on.

Exercise 2

Pitch changes the roof and not the harvest

  1. New model, and leave the block at its default 12 × 8 m footprint, gable roof, 0.5 m overhang, pitched rough surface.
  2. Set the pitch to 15°. Record catchment, surface area and annual yield: 117.00 m², 121.13 m², 51.84 m³.
  3. Take it to 25, 35, then 45°. The surface climbs to 129.10, 142.83 and 165.46 m². The catchment does not move. The yield does not move.
  4. Change the roof form to vault and set the rise to 6 m. The surface goes further still; the catchment stays at 117.00 m².
  5. Now change the overhang from 0.5 to 1.5 m and watch the catchment move for the first time.
  6. Finally switch the form to sawtooth and note that the catchment drops to 110.50 m² — the one form that carries its overhang on the low edge only.

The principle. Rain falls vertically, so the catchment is the plan projection: BS EN 16941‑1:2024 defines A as the horizontal projection of the collection area. A 45° roof buys 41 % more covering, more structure and more cost, and collects not one extra litre. If you want more water, extend the footprint or the overhang. Pitch is a decision about weathering, daylight, form and snow — never about harvest.

Exercise 3

The same rainfall, arriving at the wrong time

  1. Open Models › Arid — Dubai. In Climate, set Rainfall input to Annual and type 900 mm. The monthly shape is kept and scaled, so Dubai's distribution survives at nine times the depth.
  2. In System, tick Daily balance and leave the capacity on the basic approach. Record the harvest, the proposed capacity and the coverage rate.
  3. Repeat, exactly, with Models › Tropical — Kuala Lumpur at the same 900 mm. The building, the demand and the harvest are identical: 121.2 m³ collected against 148.2 m³ demanded in both cases.
  4. Compare the coverage rate. Dubai's distribution reaches 42.2 %; Kuala Lumpur's reaches 77.4 %.
  5. Now set the capacity manually and sweep it in both models. Dubai needs 31 871 litres to reach its knee; Kuala Lumpur needs 6 972. Four and a half times the tank, for a worse result.
  6. Look at the store-behaviour chart for each. One empties for a season; the other cycles.

The principle. Annual rainfall totals tell you almost nothing about a harvesting system. What decides the tank is the longest stretch during which demand runs ahead of supply, and two places with the same annual depth can differ by a factor of four and a half in the storage needed to bridge it. This is why the standard's basic approach is parameterised by a dry period rather than by rainfall alone, and why its detailed approach insists on a daily series. Quote the distribution, not just the total.

Everything on this page was read from the tool's source code, and the measured tables were produced by driving its own GEO and HYD modules. Where a figure is quoted it is measured, not estimated. Where the code names no source, this page says so rather than supplying one.

Claims the code makes about BS EN 16941‑1:2024 were checked clause by clause against the published standard. That check found four disagreements, all since corrected in the tool: four of its six surface coefficients sat below the Table 2 values while the code cited Table 2 as their source; the automatic tank size was fixed at 5 % of the lesser of yield and demand, where A.2.1 parameterises it by a dry period you choose and NA.3 reduces it at extreme yield-to-demand ratios; the treatment coefficient was described as a minimum the standard requires when it is a fallback the standard offers; and a step-counting reliability figure was presented alongside the standard's coverage rate without distinguishing the two. If you are working from an older copy of the tool, those four are what to re-check.

For anything that must be built, none of this substitutes for a designer who can take responsibility for the system. The tool is built to teach the relationships — catchment against surface, distribution against total, storage against reliability — and it contains no water quality, no hydraulics and no cost model at all, deliberately, so that what it does do stays legible.

  • Cite the DOI, and pick the right one. 10.5281/zenodo.22801122 is the concept DOI and resolves to the newest version — cite that for the tool. 10.5281/zenodo.22801123 is version 1.0, frozen; cite that when you report figures someone should be able to reproduce, because it names the exact code and the exact rainfall library they came from.   • Record which source your city came from, and the station. Twenty-two presets are WMO Climatological Standard Normals; six are WWIS records over other periods.   • Acknowledge the WMO World Weather Information Service if you quote one of its six cities.   • Record the version and the date you downloaded the Normals. The NCEI accession is revised as WMO Members submit corrections — this library was built from version 6.6 on 16 September 2026, and the accession has been updated since first publication. A citation without a version names a moving target.
