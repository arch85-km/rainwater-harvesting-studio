Rainwater Harvesting Studio · In brief

Method notes in brief

The short version: what the tool calculates, which clause of BS EN 16941‑1:2024 each step comes from, and where it departs from that standard. The method is the standard’s; the rainfall behind it is twelve monthly totals per city, which is the single fact that decides how far a number from here can travel. The full method notes give the clause-by-clause check, the measured tables and the provenance of every figure.

Three things to know before you cite a number

  1. There is no daily rainfall in this tool, anywhere. The standard’s detailed approach (A.2.2.2) runs on measured daily rainfall over at least five years. This tool holds twelve monthly totals per location and nothing else. Its daily balance option does not read daily data: it divides the month’s depth by a typical depth per wet day to get a count of wet days, then spreads the month’s water evenly across them. A synthetic year of identical showers stands in for a real one of downpours and dry spells — and real rainfall is lumpier, so real dry runs are longer and real spills larger than this shows. Expect it to flatter a small tank, and do not present a tank size from here as a BS EN 16941‑1 detailed-approach sizing.
  2. The number to report is mains water displaced, not annual yield. Annual yield is what the roof collects; a good deal of it overflows and is never used. Mains water displaced is what the store actually supplied — what the system is for, and what the saving is worked out from. Quote yield without storage and you will overstate the benefit, often by half.
  3. A station is not a city, and six of the twenty-eight presets are not on the same footing as the rest. Twenty-two come from the WMO Climate Normals 1991–2020 — one named gauge, one period, one definition of a rain day throughout. Six — Nairobi, Athens, Amman, Baghdad, Doha and Bogotá — come from the WMO World Weather Information Service instead, over whatever period their national service published — from 1955–1997 onwards, and two declare no period at all. docs/climate-source.json names the source, station, WMO number, elevation and period for every city. Cite the station, not the city: its London is a central gauge reading 113.5 rain days a year, and Heathrow is a different gauge 22 km west reading 111.6.

What it calculates

Yield is Formula (1) of clause 6.1.2, implemented directly and summed over surfaces so that blocks of different materials each keep their own coefficient:

Y = Σ ( A_i × e_i × h × η ) litres A_i horizontal projection of collection area i, m² e_i surface yield coefficient of that surface, Table 2 h rainfall depth for the time step, mm η hydraulic treatment efficiency coefficient 1 mm falling on 1 m² = 1 litre, which is where the units close.

Two losses, and only two. e is what the roof surface itself loses to wetting, evaporation and wind — all eight surfaces of Table 2 at the published values. η is what the pre-tank treatment loses, defaulting to 0.90. The note to clause 6.1.2 says 0.9 can be used where the manufacturer specifies nothing: it is a fallback the standard offers, not a minimum it requires. Put the manufacturer’s figure in when you have one. The first-flush input is the tool’s own addition; Formula (1) has no such term, so leave it at 0 to calculate strictly to the standard.

Demand is built per person per day from four end uses times occupancy, plus an optional seasonal irrigation term. WC flushing at 25 and laundry at 15 come to 40 l/person/day, which is exactly the BSI UK National Annex NA.1.2 figure — but the split between the two is the tool’s, and cleaning at 5 and vehicle wash at 2 have no source at all. Annual demand is daily × 365, as Formula (4). The tool always uses 365 and gives you no way to say otherwise, so a school or office model overstates annual demand by every day the building is shut.

The store is operated by Formulas (A.3) and (A.4) of Annex A.2.2.3. Abstraction is the lesser of the demand and the volume standing at the end of the previous step, before this step’s inflow is added; anything above the usable volume overflows. That is the YAS convention — Yield After Spillage — and it is the conservative one. Three warm-up cycles run before the fourth is reported, so the answer does not depend on how full the tank was assumed to be on 1 January.

Alongside it the tool reports the coverage rate Cr, supplied divided by demanded, which is Formula (A.5); and a second figure counting the steps in which demand was met in full. That second figure is the tool’s own — the standard has no such measure, and the two diverge whenever a shortfall is partial rather than total. Report Cr if you are citing the standard.

Catchment is the plan projection, and pitch does not change it

The tool builds each roof as a set of planes and keeps the vertical component of the area vector — the area of the roof’s shadow on the ground, not the area of the sloping surface you would tile. This is not a simplification the tool has chosen: BS EN 16941‑1:2024 clause 6.1.2 defines A in Formula (1) as the horizontal projection of the collection area. Rain falls vertically; a steeper roof intercepts no more of it.

The time step decides the answer

With monthly stepping there is one draw per month, taken against the volume standing at the end of the previous month. A tank cannot be drawn twice, so however often it would really refill, the most it can supply in a month is its own capacity. On the terraced-housing model, whose demand is about 22 m³ a month, that is not a small effect:

The monthly step is only safe for comparing capacities well above that threshold. A coverage rate quoted without its time step cannot be checked by anyone reading you — the step alone moves it from 4.6 % to 49.5 %.

Where the figures come from, and where they come from nowhere

Most of the method is traceable to a clause. Some of what the tool puts on screen is not, and the difference matters more than any single number:

A wet day here is 1 mm or more, which matches the Normals' own DP01 definition — but Baghdad counts a rain day at 0.001 mm and four of the six WWIS cities declare no threshold, so their depth per wet day is not comparable with the rest.
No source
Several numbers on screen
The 75 mm/h design storm and the downpipe table; cleaning at 5 and vehicle wash at 2 l/person/day, and the split of NA.1.2's 40 into WC 25 and laundry 15; the mains tariff of 2.20, whose currency the code never names; and the knee rule — the smallest capacity reaching 95 % of the best achievable coverage, where the standard plots the curve and leaves the choice to the project.

The litres-per-second figure is not a design. The gutter and downpipe check divides the catchment by one fixed storm intensity and looks a pipe up in a table, and the code names a source for neither. Its own comment marks it as not a substitute for BS EN 12056‑3. Use it to see that a roof this size needs pipes of roughly this order, and for nothing else.

Not modelled at all

None of the following is approximated or simplified. It is absent, and a result from this tool says nothing about any of it.

  • Water quality, treatment and risk assessment — clauses 5.9 and 10, and the whole of National Annex NA.2 with its guideline values and monitoring. A tool that sizes a tank has said nothing about whether the water in it is fit for the use.
  • Pumps, pipework, controls, pressure and energy. No pump curve, no head, no kWh. And no back-up supply sizing: a mains top-up volume is reported, but no pipe and no air gap.
  • Carbon, capital cost and payback. The tool multiplies displaced mains water by a tariff. That is a saving in water charges, not a financial appraisal.
  • Climate change. National Annex NA.4 asks for a sensitivity analysis on drier summers and wetter winters. The tool holds no future scenario and will not prompt you for one, so a tank sized here is sized for the climate that was.
  • Tank geometry, siting and losses. Capacity is a number of litres — no shape, no minimum water level, no calmed inlet, no evaporation or leakage. Nor where the overflow goes: the volume is reported and the model stops there.
  • Regulatory compliance of any kind. No Building Regulations, no water fittings regulations, no pass or fail, no notion of jurisdiction beyond the National Annex rules named above. Treat every output as a design study.

Three exercises

Each is a sequence of settings and a number to read back. The figures quoted were produced by running the tool’s own modules, so if you follow the steps exactly you should see them.

Exercise 01

The tank that stops paying for itself

  1. Open Models › Terraced housing row — five blocks, 325 m² of catchment, 18 occupants, Dublin rainfall.
  2. In the Demand tab, scroll to the System group. Tick Daily balance, and set Storage capacity to Set manually.
  3. Set Tank capacity to 500 litres. The tank is what you are changing; the coverage rate is what you read back, from the Demand met tile in the strip along the bottom of the window — or the row Coverage rate Cr in the Results tab, which is the same figure.
  4. Repeat for 1000, 1500, 2000, 4000 and 10000 litres, writing the coverage rate down each time. You should get 24.7 %, 49.5 %, 68.8 %, 68.8 %, 68.8 %, 68.8 %.
  5. Switch Storage capacity back to BS basic approach and read the size it proposes: 8 913 litres, against a curve that flattened at 1 500. Capacity optimisation, at the foot of the Results tab, draws that curve for you.
  6. Untick Daily balance and watch every figure collapse. Explain why before reading on.

Principle. Storage buys reliability with sharply diminishing returns, and the point of diminishing return is set by how often the tank refills — not by the annual totals. A rule of thumb worked out on annual figures cannot see that point: here it sizes the store six times larger than the curve justifies. Size from the curve, and say which time step you ran it on.

Exercise 02

Pitch changes the roof and not the harvest

  1. New model, and leave the block at its default 12 × 8 m footprint, gable roof, 0.5 m eaves overhang, rough pitched tile.
  2. In the Roof tab, set Pitch to 15° and record catchment, surface area and annual yield: 117.00 m², 121.13 m², 51.84 m³.
  3. Take the pitch to 25, 35, then 45°. The surface climbs to 129.10, 142.83 and 165.46 m². The catchment does not move. The yield does not move.
  4. Change Roof form to Vault, leaving Rise at crown at its default 3 m. The surface reaches 185.69 m², more than the 45° gable; the catchment is still 117.00 m².
  5. Set the form back to Gable and change Eaves overhang from 0.5 to 1.5 m. Now the catchment moves for the first time: 117.00 → 165.00 m². Put it back to 0.5 and switch the form to Sawtooth: the catchment drops to 110.50 m², the one form carrying its overhang on the low edge only.

Principle. Rain falls vertically, so the catchment is the plan projection, exactly as the standard defines it. A 45° roof buys 41 % more covering, more structure and more cost, and collects not one extra litre. If you want more water, extend the footprint or the overhang. Pitch is a decision about weathering, daylight, form and snow — never about harvest.

Exercise 03

Nearly the same rainfall, arriving at the wrong time

  1. Open Models › Tropical — Kuala Lumpur. In the Demand tab, scroll to the System group, tick Daily balance and leave the capacity on the basic approach. Record the harvest, the proposed capacity and the Demand met figure: 383.3 m³ collected against 148.2 m³ demanded, a proposed 3 654 litres, and coverage at 100 %.
  2. Change nothing except the city. In Climate, set Location to Chittagong. The dropdown prints each city’s annual rainfall beside its name, so you can see what you have just done: 2 847.1 mm a year becomes 2 964.0 — four per cent more rain.
  3. Record again. The roof, the demand and the tank are untouched, and the harvest has gone up to 399.1 m³. Coverage falls from 100 % to 79.5 %.
  4. Set Storage capacity to Set manually and step Tank capacity through the range in each city. Kuala Lumpur reaches 100 % at 3000 litres and stays there. Chittagong climbs slowly — 68.3 % at 1 500, 77.2 at 3 000, 83.7 at 10 000, 90.5 at 20 000 — and does not reach 100 % until 40 000 litres. Thirteen times the tank, on four per cent more rain. The knees sit at 1 353 and 17 591 litres.
  5. Look at the Storage behaviour chart in the Results tab for each. One cycles all year; the other fills once in the monsoon and empties over the five months from November to March.

Principle. Annual rainfall totals tell you almost nothing about a harvesting system. What decides the tank is the longest stretch during which demand runs ahead of supply, and two cities within four per cent of each other on annual depth can differ by a factor of thirteen in the storage needed to bridge it — with the wetter one needing the larger tank. This is why the standard’s basic approach is parameterised by a dry period rather than by rainfall alone, and why its detailed approach insists on a daily series. Quote the distribution, not just the total.

Where it stops

The tool is built to teach three relationships — catchment against surface, distribution against total, storage against reliability — and it contains no water quality, no hydraulics and no cost model at all, deliberately, so that what it does do stays legible. For anything that must be built, none of this substitutes for a designer who can take responsibility for the system.

It flatters the tank. The store is run against a made-up rainfall pattern — the month’s water shared equally between its wet days. Real rain arrives in bursts with long gaps between them, and it is the gaps that empty a tank. So the dry spells here are shorter than real ones, and less water goes over the overflow than really would. Expect a real installation to do worse than this shows, and expect the gap to be widest for the smallest tanks.

It also collects more than a real roof. Two losses are applied and no others: the surface coefficient e and the treatment coefficient η. Nothing is deducted for rain that overshoots a gutter, sits in a blocked filter, or is lost in the plumbing between the roof and the tank.

And exact arithmetic is not a right answer. The books balance to the litre — what comes in equals what is used, plus what spills, plus what is left standing in the tank. That is a property of the sums, not of the result. Every uncertainty is in what you fed them: the rainfall, the coefficients, the time step.

One thing you cannot check for yourself. BS EN 16941‑1:2024 is paywalled, so confirming what this page says about its clauses needs a purchased copy or an institutional subscription.
